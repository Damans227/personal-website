Socrates didn't tell people what to think. He asked them questions. The whole move of his was that if you asked a careful enough question, the person would walk their own belief into the open, look at it in daylight, and either keep it or change it, all without you having told them anything.

That is still, two and a half thousand years later, the single most powerful conversational move most people never make. Not for themselves, not for anyone else. They skip straight to the answer, which is where almost every argument you've ever had went wrong.

This post is the structure of the Socratic move. It works on your own thoughts, when you're stuck. It also works in conversations with other people, when they are. The structure is the same.

---

## The shape: five Rs, in a loop

Socratic dialogue is not a single clever question. It's a loop. Five steps:

```
RECEIVE   ->  listen to the actual thing being said
REFLECT   ->  mirror it back, cleanly
REFINE    ->  ask a question that opens the belief up
RESTATE   ->  let the new version come out
REPEAT    ->  go round again, deeper
```

Most people, trying to be helpful, skip to Refine. That is when it stops being dialogue and starts being an interrogation. The entire power of the method lives in how the loop *starts*, not where it goes.

---

## R1: Receive

Shut up and listen to the actual thing.

Not the gist. Not what you think they probably mean. The actual thing. The words, the tone, the whole statement. Receive is the step most people skip entirely, because listening without planning your response is harder than it sounds.

For yourself, Receive looks like this: write the thought down. The exact thought, in your own voice, without softening it. "I think ___." No analysis yet.

---

## R2: Reflect

Say it back.

"So what you're saying is X."
"Just so I've got it right, you're thinking Y."

The point of Reflect is twofold. One, you make sure you actually heard the thing. Two, the other person hears their own thought in a different voice, and that alone sometimes starts to shift it. A thought that lived unchallenged inside your head for an hour can sound slightly ridiculous when it's said back to you by another human.

For yourself, Reflect looks like this: read the sentence you just wrote out loud. Just once. Then sit with it for a second.

**Most of the real work of change happens in R1 and R2.** The questions are the famous bit, but if you have not Received and Reflected, no question lands.

---

## R3: Refine (the questions)

Now, and only now, you ask something that opens the belief up.

The source material I keep coming back to names ten of these questions. I've grouped them so you can see what each one is doing.

### Evidence questions

```
What is the evidence for this thought? What's the evidence
against it?

Am I basing this on facts, or on how I'm feeling?

Am I looking at ALL the evidence, or just what supports
my belief?

Do I think this way out of habit, or do facts actually
support it?
```

These four are the backbone. Most stuck thoughts collapse the second you ask them for receipts.

### Alternative-view questions

```
Is this thought black and white, when it's probably more
complicated?

Might someone else interpret this situation differently?
If so, how?
```

These two force a second reading of the same event. Most thoughts take the first reading as fact.

### Error-check questions

```
Could I be making an inaccurate assumption here?

Could my thinking be an exaggeration of what's actually
true?

Is this a likely outcome, or just the worst case scenario?
```

Three ways of asking: *is this reading of reality bigger than reality itself?*

### Origin question

```
Did someone pass this belief onto me? And if so, were
they a reliable source?
```

The sneakiest question in the set. A lot of what you believe about yourself wasn't your idea in the first place. It got installed by someone whose authority you probably wouldn't trust if you heard them say it now.

Pick one or two questions, not ten. The point is not to run the whole battery. The point is to loosen the belief enough that a different version becomes possible.

---

## R4: Restate

Let the new version come out.

Don't tell the person what the new version should be. Don't tell yourself, either. Just make space and see what comes.

"So what are you actually thinking now?"
"How would you say it differently now?"
"If you had to put it in a sentence, what would it be?"

The restated version is almost never the opposite of the original. It's usually softer, more specific, more honest. "They don't like me" becomes "I'm not sure where I stand with them." "I'm a failure" becomes "I failed at this one specific thing and I don't like how it felt." Both are the same person, in the same situation, with the same facts. The second one is just less of a weapon.

---

## R5: Repeat

The new version is a new belief. Which means it has its own evidence, its own assumptions, its own blind spots.

So you go round again. Receive the new version. Reflect it back. Refine it. Let a newer version come out.

```
R1 Receive     ----\
R2 Reflect         |
R3 Refine          |----- one loop
R4 Restate         |
                 --/

Then around again, with the new belief in hand.
```

Each loop goes a little deeper. Most of what's actually eating someone is three or four loops below the surface statement they walked in the door with. You don't get there with one clever question. You get there with the patience to go around four times.

---

## Yourself and other people

The exact same loop works in both directions.

**On yourself** (when a thought has you):
```
R1: Write it out. Exact words.
R2: Read it out loud.
R3: Pick one or two of the ten questions and answer honestly.
R4: Write the new version.
R5: Go again.
```

**On someone else** (when they're stuck):
```
R1: Listen to what they actually said.
R2: Say it back to them cleanly.
R3: Ask one question. Not many. One.
R4: Let them restate.
R5: Loop.
```

The thing to resist, in both directions, is **giving the answer**. The whole method stops working the moment you tell someone (or yourself) what the right conclusion is. Even if your conclusion is correct. The person has to arrive at it themselves, or the belief shift won't stick.

---

## The one rule, if you remember nothing else

> Receive and Reflect before you Refine. The question only works if the person feels heard first.

This holds for other people, obviously. It also holds for yourself. **You will not interrogate your own thought successfully if you have not first let yourself fully feel it.** Trying to argue a feeling away before you have even acknowledged it is just a sophisticated form of avoiding it. The loop starts with hearing. Everything else fails if the hearing didn't happen.

---

## A short exercise

Right now, pick one thought that's been running around your head today. Not a huge one, just one that didn't let you let it go.

```
R1. Write it, exact words:

R2. Read it out loud, once:

R3. Pick one question from the ten above, answer honestly:

R4. Write the thought again, revised:

R5. Pick a second question, answer, revise once more.
```

Fifteen minutes. That's one proper loop on one real thought. Done honestly, it will shift something you weren't expecting to shift.

---

## The one line

If you keep nothing else:

> The person who asks a careful question changes more than the person who gives a correct answer. That is true in every conversation you will ever have, including the one you have with yourself.

The Socratic move isn't knowing more. It is knowing how to hold a question long enough that the thought has to answer for itself.
