A single bad day is a bad day. A bad day repeated enough times becomes something else. It becomes a rule you did not consciously write. The rule, repeated enough times, becomes an identity you did not consciously choose.

The useful thing I keep coming back to is that this process has distinct layers, and each layer needs a different response. If you can tell which layer you are standing on, you know what to do about it. If you cannot, you spend years treating a surface thought like a deep belief, or ignoring a deep belief as if it were a surface thought.

There are three layers. Think of them as a stack.

```
+----------------------------------------------+
|  NEGATIVE AUTOMATIC THOUGHTS (NATs)          |   surface
|  Pop in uninvited. Specific to one moment.   |   noise
|  "He didn't text back. He must be annoyed."  |
+----------------------------------------------+
|  DYSFUNCTIONAL ASSUMPTIONS                   |   day-to-day
|  If-then rules you operate by.               |   shortcuts
|  "If people don't text back, they're angry." |
+----------------------------------------------+
|  CORE BELIEFS                                |   quiet
|  Verdicts about who you are. Feel like       |   verdicts
|  facts, not opinions.                        |
|  "I am not someone people want around."      |
+----------------------------------------------+
```

Top is the loud layer. Bottom is the quiet layer. The quiet one runs the show.

---

## Layer 1: Negative Automatic Thoughts

The surface noise. Hundreds a day, most of them ridiculous when you say them out loud.

- "He didn't say hi because he's mad at me."
- "That meeting is going to go badly."
- "She noticed I used the wrong word."
- "I shouldn't have eaten that."

The signature of a NAT is **reflexive, not reasoned**. You did not think it, it appeared. You cannot filter them at the door. The mind is a thought generator. That is its job.

A NAT on its own, held for 30 seconds and then dropped, costs you nothing. One of the most useful things to learn is that **a thought appearing is not the same as a thought being true**, and definitely not the same as acting on it. The window between the thought appearing and you doing something about it is the entire training ground.

**Self-check for Layer 1.** Catch one. Write the exact words of a thought you had today, without smoothing them. Then ask: "If a friend said this out loud, would it sound reasonable, or ridiculous?" About half the time, out loud, your NATs sound unhinged. That is the point. They are not supposed to be accurate. They are supposed to be fast.

---

## Layer 2: Dysfunctional Assumptions

Underneath the noise, there are rules. You wrote these without noticing. They are the if-then shortcuts your mind runs to interpret events quickly, without having to think.

- "If I don't impress people, they will stop liking me."
- "If someone criticises my work, it means I am bad at my job."
- "If I say no, I will be seen as difficult."
- "If I ask for help, people will think less of me."

The signature of an assumption is that **it feels like common sense until you examine it**. Say it out loud and most of your assumptions are not actually true, they are just one possible read of how things might work. The job of a dysfunctional assumption is to save you from thinking. The cost is that it also saves you from accuracy.

Assumptions are where most of your emotional reactions actually get made. The NAT is the spark. The assumption is why the spark catches.

**Self-check for Layer 2.** When you notice an outsized reaction, ask: "What rule would have to be true for this reaction to make sense?" You will usually find an if-then sentence sitting there. Write it down. Then ask: "Is this actually a rule, or just something I have been assuming?" Most assumptions, once you can see them, lose about half their power on the spot.

---

## Layer 3: Core Beliefs

At the bottom, the quiet verdicts. These are not thoughts you have, they are things you believe about yourself.

- "I am not good enough."
- "I am unlovable."
- "I am a burden."
- "I am going to be found out."
- "I am not safe."

The signature of a core belief is that it does not announce itself as a belief. It feels like reality. You do not notice you have the belief, because you are looking at the world *through* it. Fish do not notice water. You do not notice the belief that is doing the looking.

Core beliefs are built from repetition. One job rejection is a bad day. Three job rejections is an assumption that something is off with how you interview. Years of job rejections, held and interpreted the same way each time, is a core belief that you are not good enough for the kind of work you want to do.

**The arrow goes down, not up.** NATs harden into assumptions when a situation repeats and the same NAT keeps firing. Assumptions harden into core beliefs when enough of your life seems to confirm the assumption. The direction is always deeper.

```
A single NAT              -> noise, probably nothing
NAT repeating in a        -> starting to form an assumption
pattern of situations     ("I always ...")

Assumption confirmed      -> hardening into a core belief
over years                ("I am ...")
```

Once something is at Layer 3, it bends everything above it to confirm itself. You will unconsciously notice evidence for the belief and skip evidence against it. This is why core beliefs are stubborn. They are not individual thoughts, they are a tilt in the lens.

**Self-check for Layer 3.** The honest version. Finish this sentence out loud: "Deep down, I am ___." Whatever word jumped to mind, especially if it was unkind, is probably a core belief operating in you. Say it one more time, slowly. That one word has been editing your interpretation of every event for years.

---

## Why the layer matters: each one needs a different response

The whole point of knowing the layers is that **the move that works at one layer is wrong at another**.

```
Layer            Right response                   Wrong response
---------        -------------                    --------------
NAT              Notice it. Let it pass.          Argue with it.
                 Do not act on it.                Build a case.

Assumption       Name the if-then. Look for       Just dismiss it.
                 real counter-evidence. Rewrite   You cannot delete a
                 the rule.                        rule by ignoring it.

Core belief      Catch it in action over time.    Try to fix it in
                 Treat it as a lens, not a fact.  one conversation
                 Slow work.                       with yourself.
```

Trying to argue with every NAT is exhausting and does nothing, because another one is already on the way. Trying to fix a core belief in an afternoon is impossible, because you cannot see the lens by staring at it harder.

The leverage is at Layer 2. **Assumptions are the layer you can actually catch and rewrite.** Not easily, but doable. Catching them is what keeps NATs from stacking into fresh beliefs, and it is what loosens the beliefs you already have.

---

## How to actually use this

Pick one reaction from the last 48 hours that was bigger than the event deserved. Then run it down the stack.

```
What happened:  ________________

Layer 1 (NAT):          What exact words went through
                        your head in the moment?

Layer 2 (ASSUMPTION):   What if-then rule would need to
                        be true for that NAT to feel
                        urgent?

Layer 3 (CORE BELIEF):  What does that rule imply about
                        who you believe you are?
```

Running one reaction all the way down to Layer 3 takes about three minutes. The result is often disproportionately clarifying. The NAT looks small. The assumption looks challengeable. The core belief, once you can see it, loses some of its grip just from being seen.

Do this on one reaction a week and you will know your own stack better than most people ever do theirs.

---

## The one line

If you keep nothing else from this post, keep this:

> A thought becomes a rule when you repeat it. A rule becomes an identity when you believe it.

The upside of knowing that is simple. **The place to intervene is while something is still a rule.** NATs are too fast to catch cleanly. Core beliefs are too deep to argue with. Assumptions are the layer where change is actually available.

Catch the assumption. Rewrite it. The rest of the stack starts to shift from there.
