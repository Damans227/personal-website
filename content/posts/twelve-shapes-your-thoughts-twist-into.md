Your mind twists things. All the time. Not maliciously, just reflexively, in a surprisingly small number of specific shapes. The useful discovery, once you can see it, is that these shapes have names. And a thought with a name on it is a thought you can catch.

Without the name, the experience is a vague "I am spiralling again." With the name, the same experience becomes something very specific: *"I am catastrophising right now."* The distance between those two sentences is almost everything. The first keeps you inside the thought. The second puts a label on it, which puts you one step outside it, which is the entire first move of actually dealing with it.

There are twelve of these shapes that come up again and again. I have grouped them into five clusters so you can locate the ones that are yours.

---

## Cluster 1: Predicting things that haven't happened

The shape of these is reacting to a future that only exists in your head.

**1. Catastrophising.** You jump straight to the worst possible outcome and treat it as the most likely one.

```
Thought:   "If I send this message, they'll ignore me,
            I'll feel awful, and this whole relationship
            will quietly fall apart."

Giveaway:  You've skipped three or four less dramatic
           outcomes and gone straight to the apocalypse.

Self-check: "Am I jumping to the worst possible conclusion?"
```

**2. Fortune-telling.** You predict a specific negative outcome and act as though it's already a fact.

```
Thought:   "This interview isn't going to go well. I can
            tell. There's no point preparing properly."

Giveaway:  You've assigned a probability of 100% to
           something you have zero actual information
           about.

Self-check: "Am I predicting the future instead of waiting
             to see what happens?"
```

---

## Cluster 2: Reading minds that aren't yours to read

Both of these are the mind inventing what other people are thinking and then reacting to the invention.

**3. Mind-reading.** You decide what someone else is thinking about you, usually negative, usually with no evidence.

```
Thought:   "She didn't say much in that meeting. She
            thinks my idea was stupid."

Giveaway:  You have a strong conviction about what is
           in someone else's head, based on something
           you watched them do that could be explained
           seventeen other ways.

Self-check: "Am I jumping to conclusions about what
             other people are thinking of me?"
```

**4. Personalising.** You take something that is about the world (or about them) and quietly make it about you.

```
Thought:   "He's been quiet all evening. I must have
            done something wrong."
            (He had a bad meeting at work.)

Giveaway:  You explain other people's moods and
           behaviours primarily through you being the
           cause.

Self-check: "Am I taking an event or someone's
             behaviour too personally, or blaming myself
             and overlooking other factors?"
```

---

## Cluster 3: Painting yourself as the problem

These three bend the lens inward and make you the object of the distortion.

**5. Labelling.** You take a specific event and turn it into a verdict on who you are.

```
Thought:   "I messed that up. I'm a failure."
           (Not: "I failed at that one specific thing.")

Giveaway:  You use "I am ___" statements instead of
           "I did ___" statements. The event becomes
           an identity.

Self-check: "Am I globally putting myself down as a
             failure, worthless, or useless?"
```

**6. Disqualifying the positive.** When something good happens, you find a reason it doesn't count.

```
Thought:   "They said my work was great, but they were
            just being nice."
            "I only got the job because they had no
             other candidates."

Giveaway:  Compliments and wins never land. There is
           always an asterisk.

Self-check: "Am I discounting positive information or
             twisting a positive into a negative?"
```

**7. Emotional reasoning.** You treat how you feel as evidence for what is true.

```
Thought:   "I feel like a fraud, so I must be one."
           "I feel like they don't like me, so they
            don't."

Giveaway:  Your feelings are being used as data.
           Feelings are information about *you*, not
           about external reality.

Self-check: "Am I listening too much to my negative
             gut feelings instead of looking at the
             objective facts?"
```

---

## Cluster 4: Collapsing nuance

These three remove the middle ground and leave you with cartoon versions of complicated things.

**8. Black-and-white thinking.** Everything is either perfect or terrible, success or failure, friend or enemy. No in between.

```
Thought:   "If I don't do this perfectly, there's no
            point doing it at all."

Giveaway:  Your options collapse to two, both extreme.
           The actual answer is almost always a boring
           middle.

Self-check: "Am I thinking in extreme (all-or-nothing)
             terms?"
```

**9. Overgeneralising.** One event becomes a universal rule, usually marked by the words *always* and *never*.

```
Thought:   "I always get this wrong."
           "They never follow through."

Giveaway:  The words "always" and "never" are doing
           heavy lifting that the actual evidence
           cannot support.

Self-check: "Am I using words like 'always' and 'never'
             to draw generalised conclusions from a
             particular event?"
```

**10. Mental filtering.** You notice the one bad thing and screen out the ten good things.

```
Thought:   "The whole day was a disaster."
           (Nine things went fine. One thing didn't.)

Giveaway:  Ask yourself what else happened. There was
           more. You just filtered it out.

Self-check: "Am I focusing on the negative and
             overlooking the positive?"
```

---

## Cluster 5: Running rigid rules

These two are the shape where you've installed a rule and the rule is now hurting you.

**11. Demanding.** You run your life (or other people's) on *should*, *must*, *ought*, *have to*. The rule leaves no room for being human.

```
Thought:   "I should be further along by now."
           "They have to text back within an hour."
           "I must be productive today."

Giveaway:  The sentence contains a hard rule you did
           not consciously agree to, and failing the
           rule produces shame instead of information.

Self-check: "Am I using words like 'should', 'must',
             'ought', and 'have to' to make rigid rules
             about myself, the world, or other people?"
```

**12. Low frustration tolerance.** You tell yourself something is unbearable when it is actually just uncomfortable.

```
Thought:   "I can't handle this."
           "This is unbearable."
           (It is bearable. It's just hard.)

Giveaway:  The word "can't" when what you mean is "I
           really don't want to." A thing being
           difficult is not the same as it being
           impossible.

Self-check: "Am I telling myself something is too
             difficult or unbearable, when it's hard
             but still bearable and worth tolerating?"
```

## How to actually use this

Don't try to catch all twelve. You can't. Pick the two or three that stung as you read them. The ones that felt sort of distant and philosophical are not yours. The ones that made you flinch a little are.

```
My top three distortions:

1. _________________________________________________

2. _________________________________________________

3. _________________________________________________
```

Then do this for a week: whenever you catch yourself in a bad spiral, say the name of the distortion out loud (or write it down). Just the name. "I'm catastrophising." "I'm mind-reading." "I'm personalising."

Nothing else. No arguing with yourself, no trying to fix the thought, no gratitude journaling. Just the name. **The naming is the whole move.** The distortion loses most of its grip the moment you can see it as a specific shape instead of as "reality."

After a week of this you will have a very clear sense of which of your twelve is doing the most damage. That is the one worth working on.

---

## The one line

If you keep nothing else from this post, keep this:

> You cannot catch what you cannot name. Name it, and you're already standing one step outside it.

Your mind will keep producing these shapes. That is what minds do. The practice is not to stop having them, which is impossible. The practice is to recognise the shape as it forms, say its name, and watch it quietly lose power as a result.
