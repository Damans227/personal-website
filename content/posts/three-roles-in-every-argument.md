Have you ever noticed that when you're in a bad argument, you keep switching sides? One minute you're the one being wronged. Next minute you're the one doing the wronging. Five minutes later you're trying to fix the whole thing. And then it loops.

That isn't random. There are three roles, and in a bad argument you cycle through all three of them, sometimes within the same five minutes. Once you can see the three roles, you can see yourself from above, and that is most of the way out.

---

## The three roles

```
                    PERSECUTOR
                    "It's your fault.
                     Why can't you just..."
                        /       \
                       /         \
                      /           \
                     /             \
                    /               \
               VICTIM  <-------->  RESCUER
               "Poor me.           "Let me help.
                I can't."           You can't do it
                                    without me."
```

**Victim.** *"This is happening TO me. I can't do anything about it. Why does this always happen to me."* The victim role feels bad. It also feels oddly safe, because from the victim seat you don't have to try, and you can't be blamed for anything you didn't do.

**Persecutor.** *"It's YOUR fault. Why can't you just do the thing I asked. You're always like this."* The persecutor role gives you a sense of power and righteousness. You get to be the one holding the standard. You get to blame.

**Rescuer.** *"Don't worry, I'll handle it. Let me do it for you. You can't manage this on your own."* The rescuer role feels generous. It is often actually about control. **The rescuer needs you to need them.** If the victim ever stands on their own, the rescuer loses their job, their sense of usefulness, and the quiet thing propping up their self-worth. So the rescuer, often without knowing it, keeps the victim slightly stuck.

All three roles have one thing in common: **none of them is taking responsibility for themselves.** The victim is waiting to be saved. The persecutor is pointing at someone else. The rescuer is managing other people's lives instead of their own.

---

## The spin

Here's the uncomfortable part. In a single argument you don't just play one role. You play all three.

Start as the rescuer. *"Here, let me help you with that."* The other person doesn't appreciate it, or does it their own way. You feel used.

You slide into victim. *"After everything I do for you, this is what I get."*

The other person pushes back. You snap. Now you're the persecutor. *"You never listen. You're always like this."*

They go quiet. You feel bad for snapping. You slide back to rescuer. *"I'm sorry, let me make it up to you."* And the loop starts again.

Three roles. One person. Thirty minutes.

---

## Families and relationships

The triangle gets really sticky in close relationships, because the roles start locking in.

- One partner is the chronic rescuer. The other partner is the chronic victim. The rescuer complains constantly about having to carry everything, but the moment the victim starts standing on their own, the rescuer feels threatened and finds a new way to help them.
- One parent is the chronic persecutor. One kid is the chronic victim. The other parent becomes the chronic rescuer, running interference. Everyone is miserable. Nobody can get off the triangle because the whole family's identity is built around it.
- At work: there's always "the problem person" (victim), the manager who's "fed up with them" (persecutor), and the colleague who "sticks up for them" (rescuer). Different week, same cast.

The roles are complementary. **A rescuer needs a victim.** A persecutor needs someone to persecute. A victim needs a rescuer or a persecutor to keep the story going. When one person steps off, it destabilises everyone else, and they'll often pull hard to drag that person back on.

---

## The triangle at group scale

The same triangle shows up at group scale, which is where it gets really sticky.

A guru who needs an audience of broken people to feel important. A boss who positions himself as the only person who can fix the team's problems, and then quietly makes sure the team never actually solves them. A religious or wellness leader whose authority depends on his audience staying wounded. A political movement where the followers are the eternal victims, the enemy is the eternal persecutor, and the leader is the eternal rescuer.

```
LEADER (chronic rescuer)
    needs
       FOLLOWERS (chronic victims)
          who need an
             ENEMY (chronic persecutor)
                who justifies the
                   LEADER
```

The giveaway, every time, is this: **does the leader actually want the followers to not need them anymore?** If the answer is no, you're looking at a dependency culture, which is just the drama triangle wearing a bigger costume. The healthiest teachers, therapists, bosses and parents all share one feature: they are trying to work themselves out of the job.

---

## The sneaky part: persecutors see themselves as victims

Almost nobody who is behaving as a persecutor thinks of themselves as the persecutor. In their own head, they are the real victim. *"I only snap because they never listen."* *"I only criticise because nobody else will tell them the truth."* *"If they didn't make me so angry, I wouldn't act this way."*

That's the triangle doing its work. It gives everyone a story where they are the wronged party. Nobody is ever the bad guy in their own head. Which is exactly why the loop keeps running.

---

## Getting off the triangle

You don't fix the triangle by trying harder in your current role. A better rescuer still needs a victim. A gentler persecutor is still blaming. A braver victim is still waiting.

You get off by doing one thing: **asking what you're responsible for here, and doing that, and nothing else.**

```
INSTEAD OF VICTIM:  "What can I actually do about this,
                     even if it's small?"

INSTEAD OF PERSECUTOR:  "What am I asking for, and can
                         I ask for it without blame?"

INSTEAD OF RESCUER:  "Is this mine to carry, or am I
                      taking it so I can feel useful?"
```

These are not comfortable questions. They take the drama out, and sometimes the drama was doing something for you. The victim gets sympathy. The persecutor gets power. The rescuer gets to feel needed. Giving those up is a real loss, even when the thing you're giving up is also the thing making you miserable.

But you can't be on the triangle AND off it. You pick one.

---

## A short self-check

Next time you're in the middle of something going sideways with another person, pause and ask yourself:

```
1. Which role am I in right now?
   (Victim? Persecutor? Rescuer?)

2. Have I been in a different role in this same
   conversation already today?

3. What would the "off the triangle" version of
   me say in this moment?
```

You won't get it right the first few times. The roles are muscle memory, and most of us have been running them since childhood. But just noticing which role you're in is the thing. The noticing is already half the exit.

---

## The one line

If you keep nothing else from this post, keep this:

> In a bad argument, you'll play all three roles. The way out isn't to play your favourite one better. It's to step off the triangle entirely.

Nobody wins on the triangle. The game is rigged against everyone on it, including you.
