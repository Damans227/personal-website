The problem with asking yourself hard questions about your own thinking is that you are the person asking *and* the person being asked. Both of them want the same answer. So the honest version of self-inquiry, done alone, tends to collapse into whatever you already thought.

The courtroom metaphor is a workaround.

In a court, nobody expects the defence and the prosecution to agree. They are *supposed* to argue opposite sides. The whole mechanism of the trial is that forcing both sides to make the strongest version of their case produces a verdict more honest than either side alone could reach.

You can do the same thing inside your own head. Four steps.

---

## 1. The Thought

Write it down. The exact thought, in your own voice, without softening it.

```
"They're mad at me."
"I'm bad at this."
"Nobody wants me there."
"Uncertainty is bad."
```

One sentence. Specific. Honest. The thought you were actually having, not the version you'd say out loud to a friend.

---

## 2. The Defence

Now put on the defence attorney's wig. Your job: build the strongest possible case *for* this thought being true.

What evidence do you have that supports it? Actual events. Things that were said. Things that happened. Patterns you've noticed. Not feelings. The defence has to argue in facts, because that is what a court admits.

```
The Thought:   "They're mad at me."

Defence:
- They haven't texted back in two days.
- They were quiet at dinner on Friday.
- Last time something like this happened,
  they were upset with me.
- They cancelled lunch on Tuesday.
```

Make the real case. If you half-build this, the whole exercise falls apart, because then the verdict is a foregone conclusion.

---

## 3. The Prosecution

Now switch wigs. You are now the prosecution. Your job: build the strongest possible case *against* the thought.

Same rules. Facts, not feelings.

```
The Thought:   "They're mad at me."

Prosecution:
- They were working on a big deadline this week.
- They've been quiet with other people too.
- When I asked, they said "all good, just busy."
- Last time I thought they were mad, they weren't.
- They initiated the plan for next weekend.
```

Notice what happens here. The prosecution is almost never *missing*. It just wasn't getting the microphone. The defence took the whole stage and never had to argue against anyone.

---

## 4. The Verdict

Now sit as the judge. Not as the defendant. Not as the prosecutor. **The judge.**

What would a reasonable person, looking at both sides of the evidence above, actually decide? Not "what feels true." What a judge, with no stake in the outcome, would conclude.

```
Verdict:  "Evidence is mixed. There is some reason to
          think they might be a bit distant, but far
          more evidence that they are busy and that my
          original reading was shaped by how I was
          feeling that day. Case dismissed, pending
          one honest conversation."
```

The verdict usually looks like that. Not a dramatic reversal. A quieter version of the original thought, with the exaggeration removed. **Case dismissed, pending one honest conversation** is often where this exercise actually lands.

---

## Why this works when self-questioning doesn't

Asking yourself "is this thought really true?" rarely gets honest answers, because the part of you asking is the same part that produced the thought. It has every reason to say yes.

But *you cannot both prosecute and defend without noticing the gap.* The exercise forces you to find the counter-evidence by giving you an explicit role whose job is to find it. The role does the work. You don't have to summon the honesty from nowhere.

Think of it as a workaround for the fact that your brain plays both sides of its own tennis match and keeps winning.

---

## Try it on one thought right now

```
The Thought:

__________________________________________________

The Defence (evidence for, facts only):

__________________________________________________

The Prosecution (evidence against, facts only):

__________________________________________________

The Verdict:

__________________________________________________
```

Ten minutes with a notebook. Pick a thought that's been running around in your head today, run it through all four slots. The verdict is almost always more measured than the original thought. That gap is the exercise.

---

## It works beautifully on kids

A thing I didn't expect about this one: it is quietly perfect for children. If a kid comes home from school with *"little Jimmy was mean, he's a bad person"*, you can walk them through it exactly the same way. Defence: what did Jimmy do? Prosecution: what else happened today? Did Jimmy do anything that wasn't mean? Verdict: what does a reasonable judge decide about Jimmy?

Kids take to the courtroom frame immediately. They've seen it in TV shows. The language of judge, defence, prosecution, verdict is already in their head. It teaches them the whole mechanism of looking at both sides before landing on a conclusion, in a shape they can actually hold.

If you're a parent, this is one of the lowest-friction ways I've seen to introduce self-awareness without ever using the words "self-awareness."

---

## The one line

If you keep nothing else from this post, keep this:

> The thought feels true because only the defence got to speak. Make the prosecution speak too, and the verdict usually changes.

Your mind is a courtroom where only one side ever shows up. The move is to make the other side show up. Everything else follows.
